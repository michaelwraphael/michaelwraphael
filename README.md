## Cognitive Sociology of AI

My work begins with a deceptively simple problem: **what must a problem become before it can be intelligently solved?**

Artificial intelligence is usually approached through models, capabilities, benchmarks, agents, alignment, or automation. I approach it sociologically—from the organization of problem-solving itself.

Every intelligent system embodies assumptions about what information is, which distinctions matter, what can be decomposed, what must remain ambiguous, how far inquiry should continue, what counts as an adequate answer, and when search should stop. These are not only technical decisions. They are theories of intelligence made operational.

The sociology of AI therefore concerns more than what artificial intelligence does to society. It asks how intelligence itself becomes socially and cognitively organized.

---

## Intelligence & Intelligibility

I treat **intelligibility** as constitutive rather than supplementary to intelligence.

A problem does not arrive already divided into the correct variables, relations, procedures, and stopping conditions. Those distinctions emerge through a course of problem-solving. What counts as relevant information depends upon how the problem is constituted; how the problem is constituted changes what can count as a solution.

This means that intelligence cannot be understood solely as successful computation over a fixed representation.

For sufficiently complex problems, intelligence includes the capacity to transform the representation through which the problem itself becomes intelligible.

**Intelligence is therefore not simply calculation within a problem-space. It is also the organization through which a problem-space becomes calculable.**

## The Cognitive Sociology of Expertise

A central distinction in my work is between **skill** and **expertise**.

### The Inverse Law of Expertise

**Skill ⊂ Expertise, while Expertise ⊄ Skill**

Skill and expertise are not simply lower and higher quantities of the same capacity.

**Skill** predominates where a problem can be responsibly decomposed into stable operations without destroying what makes the problem intelligible. Procedural rationality works because the relevant relations survive decomposition.

**Expertise** becomes necessary where decomposition is itself consequential—where components acquire meaning through their relations, relevant information emerges during inquiry, and specifying the complete sequence of operations in advance would prematurely determine the very problem that must be discovered.

Expertise therefore involves **heuristic search, progressive deepening, deferred specification, and the preservation of consequential ambiguity**.

The distinction matters for AI because increasing the sophistication of procedural execution does not necessarily produce expertise. A system may become extraordinarily capable at performing specified operations while remaining unable to recognize when the specification itself has become the central object of inquiry.

## Cognitive Style, Expertise & Authority

Expertise is not a permanent property possessed by a particular class of people, and authority does not simply reside in an expert, a credential, or a problem considered as a sovereign object.

Authority becomes intelligible through a **socially situated cognitive style of problem-solving**: through the organization of attention, heuristic search, ambiguity, communication, audience participation, inference, and continuation.

An expertise-oriented cognitive style does more than produce an answer. It preserves enough of the organization of the problem that another participant can recover why the answer follows, contest the distinctions through which it was produced, and continue the inquiry where necessary.

This is where questions of cognition become questions of social organization.

Who gets to define the problem?

Whose distinctions become consequential?

Whose uncertainty is treated as thoughtfulness and whose as incompetence?

When does continued inquiry count as expertise—and when is it dismissed as overthinking?

Who has the institutional capacity to impose a stopping condition?

These questions form part of what I call the **politics of expertise**.

## AI as a Sociology of Problem-Solving

AI makes these questions unusually concrete because assumptions that can remain tacit in human institutions must increasingly be represented in computational form.

A system must somehow determine:

- what constitutes the current problem;
- which distinctions remain relevant;
- which information is fixed and which remains immanent;
- what can safely be decomposed;
- how prior activity constrains the present move;
- when uncertainty should trigger deeper search;
- how audiences and downstream consequences enter calculation;
- which transformations preserve the identity of the problem;
- and when an answer is sufficiently intelligible to terminate inquiry.

Each answer constitutes a particular organization of intelligence.

For me, this makes AI an extraordinary site for cognitive sociology: **theories of cognition, expertise, organization, authority, and problem-solving are becoming executable.**

The sociological question is no longer only how people understand intelligence.

It is increasingly **which understanding of intelligence gets built.**

## Design ↔ Discourse

Two concepts organize much of how I approach that problem.

### **Design**

The production of determinate structures through which a problem can become actionable: distinctions, representations, rules, procedures, interfaces, formal relations, and computational objects.

### **Discourse**

The recursive course through which those structures acquire meaning: interpretation, articulation, contestation, correction, audience participation, and continued problem-solving.

Neither is sufficient by itself.

**Design without discourse can freeze a provisional representation into the apparent structure of reality.**

**Discourse without design can remain indefinitely articulable without acquiring the determinate form required for action.**

Intelligence develops through their recursive relation.

**Design ⇄ Discourse**

## What I'm Doing on GitHub

I use computational artifacts as a medium of sociological inquiry.

Code can do something unusual for social theory: it forces concepts to encounter their consequences.

A distinction that sounds persuasive in prose may become incoherent when it has to govern an operation. A theory of intelligence may reveal an unnoticed stopping condition when instantiated as a procedure. A conception of authority may behave very differently once something must determine which actions are actually permitted. An account of memory, interpretation, or expertise must eventually answer the question: **what changes when this distinction is taken seriously?**

That is the territory I am exploring here.

Not sociology applied after the technology has been built.

**Sociology participating in the constitution of what intelligence can become.**
