## The Cognitive Sociology of Expertise

My work begins from a distinction between **skill** and **expertise**. Skill forms part of expertise. Expertise has additional requirements that become visible when a problem cannot be responsibly reduced to a stable procedure.

### The Inverse Law of Expertise

**Skill ⊂ Expertise, while Expertise ⊄ Skill**

Skill is suited to problems that can be responsibly decomposed into stable procedures without losing the relations that make the problem intelligible. Expertise becomes necessary with quasi-decomposable problems. Relevant information emerges through inquiry, and decomposition can change what the problem means. The next useful specification cannot always be known in advance.

Expertise depends on heuristic search and progressive deepening. No sequence specified in advance can fully determine the course. As the work proceeds, attention shifts and information changes relevance; even the standard for an adequate solution can change.

## Cognitive Style, Intelligibility, and Authority

I use **cognitive style** to describe the socially situated orientation through which problem-solving is organized. It concerns the treatment of information and ambiguity, the depth of inquiry, and the articulation of a proposed solution to others.

**Intelligibility** concerns whether the work of problem-solving can be understood, evaluated, and continued by others. A result acquires its meaning through that course of activity and through its relation to the people who must make sense of it.

My current formulation is that **authority is derived through the cognitive style enacted in a course of problem-solving**. Credential, position, confidence, and status are analytically distinct from this source of authority. The authority at issue here emerges through the organization and articulation of problem-solving itself.

## Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence intersects with this research because AI systems increasingly participate in problem-solving, judgment, and decision-making. Their operation depends on assumptions about how information is represented, what counts as relevant, how a problem can be decomposed, how search should proceed, and when it should stop.

The sociology of AI extends the sociology of expertise into cases where particular ways of organizing intelligence are implemented as computational systems that participate in social life.

I study the consequences of that intersection for expertise and authority inside organizations and professions, along with wider judgments of competence, failure, and decision-making.

## Research

My research follows problem-solving across domains where participants have to decide what matters, how far inquiry should proceed, whether a representation is adequate, and what gives a proposed solution authority.

### Methods and Research Design

My methodological work includes ethnographic and visual fieldwork, qualitative content analysis, experimental task design, measurement, coding, quantitative analysis, and methods teaching. At CUNY, I taught both **Introduction to Research Methods** and **Advanced Methods of Social Research**. In those courses, research design meant working through how a sociological question becomes observable, how evidence is constituted, what a measure represents, and what a particular design permits a researcher to claim.

My early field research included ethnographic and photographic work in and around Belfast and Dublin through Northeastern University and Queen's University Belfast, where I gathered preliminary material for a larger study of policing in Ireland and Northern Ireland. My master's research approached informal interaction through qualitative content analysis, beginning with a sample of 50 self-help books on interpersonal relationships and continuing with a systematic pilot study of a selected subsample. A separate visual project developed over several years of twilight and horizon photography and eventually became *The Politics of Twilights*.

For the doctoral project, I built a larger empirical apparatus around a 38-page legal-reasoning task using rankings, Likert scales, categorical choices, paired passages, and open-response questions. I constructed a codebook linking questions, variables, experimental tasks, indices, measurement levels, formulas, and crosswalks. The respondent-level administration, coding, and analysis workflow preserved 147 pseudonymous response IDs across uncoded, coded, binary-coded, transformed, thematic, and summary layers.

The quantitative design specified descriptive statistics and composite measures together with Spearman rank-order correlations, biserial correlations, and chi-square tests. These procedures were intended to examine relationships among socio-political attitudes, educational background, everyday problem-solving, specialized legal reasoning, tolerance of interpretation, and approaches to teaching legal reasoning.

The difficulty arose before the statistical analysis could settle anything. Making cognitive style calculable first required rendering an ongoing course of activity as observations, variables, values, categories, indices, and bounded occasions. Rendering the activity this way made disciplined comparison possible and fixed relations that the experimental task itself kept changing as respondents moved among different roles, audiences, objects, and forms of accountability. Later explanations could qualify earlier categorical responses; a change of role could alter what counted as a relevant fact or an adequate answer; a later judgment could change the meaning of an earlier one.

At that point, the empirical project became methodologically reflexive. The instrument, codebook, and successive layers of coding recorded the object through progressively more stabilized representations, and each stabilization made the constitution of the empirical object newly visible. Statistical calculation remained valid within the design. It could not establish whether the design exhausted the activity from which its variables had been abstracted. The study was completed during the dissertation project and was not separately published. Its empirical component was not included in the final dissertation manuscript; the committee concluded that it was unnecessary to the dissertation's argument. I carried that methodological problem into my later work: computational efficacy depends on a prior representational design, and the adequacy of that design still has to be examined.

### Cognitive Sociology, Expertise, and Social Theory

My work in cognitive sociology began with the problem of how sociology conceptualizes cognition. In [*Cognitive Sociology*](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_pubs/284/) (Oxford Bibliographies, 2017), I organized the field around five ideal-typical ways social theory understands cognition. The bibliography brings together work on knowledge, rationality, embodiment, practice, discourse, and tacit knowledge so that the different images of cognition can be compared while their differences remain visible.

Mapping the field left a different problem open. In [*Theorizing, Bounded Rationality, and Expertise*](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_pubs/735/) (Current Perspectives in Social Theory, 2022), theorizing is itself an activity performed for an audience while a problem is being worked out. I use Herbert Simon's bounded rationality and the division of cognitive labor to examine explanation under those conditions. **Representational representationality** names one part of the problem. The relation among language, meaning, and thought is affected by what an audience knows, what remains obscure, and how an explanation is being communicated. A formally adequate inference can still fail in a course of theorizing when the relation it is supposed to make intelligible has not become intelligible to the people who must continue from it.

Once an audience has to use a proposed solution, the stopping point itself becomes part of the sociological problem. What the solution means and what can be done with it depend in part on the people who must continue from it, so completion of a procedure cannot by itself settle when search should stop. An asserted solution can repose the problem for the people who receive it. In my dissertation, [*The Politics of Expertise and the Articulation of Authority in Democracy*](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_etds/5353/), two relations organize the analysis: the **immanence of information** to the ongoing course of problem-solving and the **articulation of authority** in the presentation of a solution.

The immanence of information means that problem-solving uses data supplied from outside the activity together with information constituted during the activity itself. References change, ambiguities appear, and an actual or projected audience can change what becomes relevant. The articulation of authority concerns the moment at which a provisional resolution is presented with enough situated certainty to guide further activity even though the course that made it intelligible cannot be reduced to the final statement.

Differences in the organization of search produce the distinction between skill-oriented and expertise-oriented problem-solving. Skill can operate within a representation whose relevant relations have been stabilized well enough for procedural rationality to guide performance. Expertise includes those capacities while remaining answerable to the possibility that the representation itself has become part of the problem. This is the asymmetry expressed by the inverse law of expertise: **expertise includes skill while skill does not include expertise**.

At organizational scale, the asymmetry concerns the distribution of cognitive labor. Institutions provide representations through which work can proceed and accredit particular ways of solving problems. Institutional arrangements can support skill very effectively. Expertise becomes consequential when continuing the inquiry requires participants to reconsider the representation, the depth of search, or the conditions under which a conclusion has become intelligible. My dissertation follows those relations through legal education, professional decision-making, organizational design, democratic participation, and artificial intelligence. My current formulation is that **authority is derived through the cognitive style enacted in a course of problem-solving**.

### Artificial Intelligence, Health, and Medicine

I began writing directly about artificial intelligence before moving into professional model evaluation. [*The Situational Rationality of Task Performance: Artifacts of Belief in Human Problem-Solving and Artificial Intelligence*](https://doi.org/10.31235/osf.io/yt9vz) (SocArXiv, 2021) approaches **artificiality** through the representations that make task performance possible. The paper compares symbolic, sub-symbolic, and hybrid configurations as distinct problem-solving forms.

In the symbolic configuration, the problem is represented as something like a maze whose elements and permissible operations can be specified. The sub-symbolic configuration instead relies on an ecological image learned through statistical regularities. Both can produce what I call natural artifacts: representations whose relevant boundaries are treated as external to the ongoing activity in which they are used. The hybrid configuration introduces socio-cognitive artifacts, for which discourse and the changing intelligibility of the problem enter the activity that produces the representation.

A representation does more than carry information into a calculation. It helps establish what can count as information for that calculation. Artificiality also includes the momentary beliefs through which complexity is recognized, features are selected, and one organization of the task becomes available for calculation while other possible organizations fall outside the representation.

I moved the analysis into medicine in [*Artificial Intelligence and the Situational Rationality of Diagnosis: Human Problem-Solving and the Artifacts of Health and Medicine*](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_pubs/734/) (Sociology Compass, 2022). Diagnosis makes the issue concrete: symptoms and signs acquire diagnostic significance through a doctor-patient encounter. The article compares procedural, probabilistic, and situational rationalities in medical AI. Symbolic systems organize diagnosis through explicit rules; sub-symbolic systems learn statistical regularities in an ecological image; a situational account treats diagnosis as a socio-cognitive artifact whose intelligibility is produced through patient-centered decision-making.

Before output accuracy can be evaluated, symptoms have to become data through some abstraction. Relevant information has to be selected, communication changes what is available for judgment, and inquiry eventually has to stop. A model can calculate correctly over the representation it has been given while the adequacy of that representation remains a separate problem. In medical AI, the representational design and the computational result cannot be evaluated as though the first were merely a neutral input to the second.

### Law, Professions, and Organizations

Law has been one of the principal domains through which I have studied expertise because legal education makes the organization of professional judgment unusually visible. Students are taught specialized ways of reading cases, classifying facts, constructing arguments, handling indeterminacy, and deciding what counts as a legally adequate reason. Professional institutions then accredit those forms of competence for use in situations where the available information may be incomplete, contested, or open to interpretation.

Much of my graduate research examined how that competence is produced. I presented work on indeterminacy in legal education, methods of constructing legal competence, narratives of "thinking like a lawyer," socio-legal pedagogy, justice, and the relation between professional identity and expertise. The doctoral empirical study grew out of the same line of inquiry. Its legal-reasoning tasks placed respondents in changing practical and interpretive positions so that everyday problem-solving could be compared with specialized legal reasoning across differences in education, socio-political attitudes, tolerance of interpretation, and approaches to teaching legal reasoning.

Legal education is especially important to my dissertation because a profession cannot reproduce expertise by supplying every future answer in advance. It has to reproduce ways of organizing attention and inquiry that can continue into cases whose relevant information has not yet been fully specified. Accreditation verifies procedural competence and establishes which forms of judgment will be recognized as competent when the representation of the problem remains unsettled.

The division of cognitive labor is visible in the artifacts professional organizations supply: rules, categories, records, routines, precedents, and institutional roles. Professional work depends on them. Rules, categories, records, routines, precedents, and institutional roles do not settle every quasi-decomposable problem that arises through their use. Expertise enters when a professional has to recognize that the current organization of those resources is insufficient and continue the work after the inherited representation stops being enough.

My earlier policing research approached professional categories and occupational practice from another direction. [*A Motion to Reconsider the “Police” Signifier*](https://www.routledge.com/Democratic-Policing-in-a-Changing-World/Manning/p/book/9781594515460) appeared as the epilogue to Peter K. Manning's *Democratic Policing in a Changing World*, and, with Manning, [*Police métier*](https://michaelwraphael.ws.gc.cuny.edu/publications/) examined policing as an occupation. These projects preceded the dissertation's mature vocabulary of cognitive style, but they already placed professional categories, occupational practice, institutional legitimacy, and public judgment in the same empirical field.

### Criminology, Policing, and Social Interaction

My research in criminology began at Northeastern University and included work with Peter K. Manning and Michael E. Brown. In 2009, I gathered preliminary ethnographic and photographic data in Belfast and Dublin for a larger study of policing in Ireland and Northern Ireland. At the time, this was criminological fieldwork. Its connection to my later work on expertise came through the broader problem of how institutional practices become intelligible to participants and observers.

With Manning, I co-authored [*Phenomenological Theories of Crime*](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_studentpubs/27/) (Oxford Bibliographies Online in Criminology, 2012). The project treats phenomenological criminology as a field with explicit epistemological and ontological commitments. It examines how crime is known, how actors' practical meanings enter explanation, how order is indicated and sustained in situations designated as criminal, and how the investigator's own claim to objectivity enters the research problem. The category of crime itself remains an empirical and theoretical problem.

My master's thesis, [*Learning Informal Interaction: The Problem of Self-Help Literature for Expression Games*](https://hdl.handle.net/2047/d20003073) (2013), studied a different problem of social competence. The instruction "just act natural" crystallized the difficulty. Explicit advice attempts to make tacit interaction available as a rule, while following the rule can change the interaction in which the conduct is supposed to appear natural. I analyzed the informal socialization of expression games, conducted a content analysis of 50 self-help books on interpersonal relationships, and carried out a systematic pilot study of a selected subsample and reader reactions.

The thesis predates the dissertation's later vocabulary. What carried forward was the relation it exposed: a representation designed to guide conduct can alter the activity it is supposed to represent. Making the instruction more explicit does not resolve the relation when the instruction itself changes what participants are doing. I later encountered the same relation in work on tacit knowledge, formalization, expertise, and the limits of treating an ongoing course of activity as a set of instructions.

### Visual Sociology, Semiotics, and Representation

My work on representation has also taken a visual and field-based form. [*The Politics of Twilights: Notes on the Semiotics of Horizon Photography*](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_pubs/538/) was published as the **lead article** in *Visual Studies* and grew out of data gathered over several years of photographic fieldwork. The article addresses a methodological problem in visual sociology: some of the meanings that organize visual practice are indexical and become difficult to recover when the practice is translated entirely into verbal description.

The article reconstructs the semiotic code of the **horizon** from the practical activity of photographing twilight. The horizon organizes the photographer's position, subject matter, light, composition, and aesthetic judgment. Photographers use it to anticipate the possibility of "capturing" light and to organize the image through which the scene will later become culturally recognizable as twilight. The finished photograph preserves the outcome of those decisions without preserving their course in the same form.

Automation made the issue more explicit. A camera can automate exposure, focus, processing, and other operations involved in producing an image. The cultural significance of the photograph still depends on decisions about what deserves representation, how the scene is organized, and what the resulting image is taken to mean. The article uses photography as both an object of sociological analysis and a mode of ethnographic inquiry into cultural meaning, narrative, and decision-making.

The fieldwork later became relevant to the dissertation because twilight changes while it is being observed. The photographer cannot wait for the scene to become a fixed object before deciding how to represent it, because the conditions that make the image possible are changing during the act of observation. Relevance, framing, adequacy, and the point at which further search should stop have to be worked out while the situation continues.

The dissertation later generalized the instability concealed by the finished photograph. A proposed solution can appear as a stable representation even though the information, judgments, and relations through which it became intelligible were constituted during the course of problem-solving. *Twilights* predates the mature theory of expertise; the fieldwork confronted me with a problem of representation and situated judgment that the dissertation later had to explain.

### Chess, Attention, and Problem-Solving

Chess was one of my earliest empirical routes into cognitive sociology and bounded rationality. My undergraduate thesis, [*Chess: The Preface to a Technical Resource for Sociology*](https://hdl.handle.net/2047/d20002591), combined sustained participation in chess with original archival research in the John G. White Collection at the Cleveland Public Library. I examined the chess-life metaphor across historical and modern narratives and treated chess as a sociological object in its own right, with particular attention to learning, representation, and the organization of competence.

The practical experience behind that work included tournament play, online play, repertoire-building, tactical training, positional analysis, coaching, instruction, and tournament direction. Chess made the gap between a complete representation and an unfinished search unusually easy to see. A position can be completely specified on the board while the organization of attention remains unresolved. The player still has to decide which relations matter, which candidate moves deserve search, how deeply a line should be calculated, when to return to the initial position, and when enough has been understood to act.

[*ReViewing Chess*](https://www.goodreads.com/author/show/4523222.Michael_W_Raphael), the 231-volume Kindle series I published in 2010–2011, grew from the same practical problem. Traditional chess books often interleave dense notation, sparse diagrams, and explanatory prose. I used the relative simplicity of the Kindle format and an interactive "guess the move" structure to direct attention back toward the represented position. I later came to understand that design as an empirical test of the structure of attention: changing the representation changed what the learner could practice and what features of the position became available for thought.

The dissertation explicitly identifies chess as the starting point of my work on cognitive sociology and bounded rationality. A fully specified position still leaves a course of search unresolved. Rules, procedures, and stored patterns can organize part of the activity. They cannot determine in advance which ambiguity will matter, how far inquiry should proceed, or what will count as sufficient understanding in the position at hand.

The chess problem led me into Herbert Simon's work on heuristic search and progressive deepening. Chess requires repeated movement between pursuing a line and returning to the position from which the line was selected. Reconsidering the position can change which line deserves deeper search, so the depth of inquiry changes with the organization of search itself. My later work on skill and expertise grows from these questions about selective attention, search, and the conditions under which inquiry halts.
